# Research Design

This scaffold supports research-methods demonstration for studying authority and power as institutional, relational, ethical, and governance conditions rather than individual influence scores, loyalty measures, political rankings, or surveillance indicators.

## Research question

How can authority effectiveness, governance risk, formal legitimacy, procedural fairness, informational access, practical influence, ethical credibility, accountability quality, voice access, oversight strength, arbitrariness risk, governance fragmentation, distrust pressure, informal power concentration, shadow-governance risk, and legitimacy decay be represented, simulated, and reviewed as unit-level and institutional constructs?

## Conceptual assumptions

1. Authority is the legitimate right to decide, direct, and hold responsibility.
2. Power is the broader capacity to influence outcomes, shape interpretation, and affect behavior.
3. Legitimacy depends on procedural fairness, competence, accountability, ethical restraint, and institutional purpose.
4. Informal power is unavoidable, but it becomes risky when hidden from accountability.
5. Governance risk rises when authority is arbitrary, fragmented, distrusted, or disconnected from practical influence.
6. Authority/power analytics must support institutional learning and governance improvement rather than worker scoring, surveillance, or dissent tracking.

## Synthetic modeling aims

The scaffold demonstrates how to:

- generate authority effectiveness scores
- estimate governance risk under authority-system conditions
- identify shadow-governance risk and legitimacy-decay risk
- summarize unit-level authority/governance review priorities
- model accountability, voice, oversight, arbitrariness, fragmentation, and distrust
- document responsible-use boundaries

## Appropriate interpretation

Outputs should be interpreted as prompts for governance, leadership, oversight, decision-rights, and institutional-learning inquiry. A high-risk synthetic profile should not be read as a real-world diagnosis. In actual organizational research, quantitative signals require qualitative validation through governance audit, decision-rights mapping, information-flow review, leadership interviews, employee voice review, psychological safety review, conflict analysis, and participatory interpretation with affected groups.
