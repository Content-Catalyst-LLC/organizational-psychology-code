# Research Design

This scaffold supports research-methods demonstration for studying job satisfaction and organizational commitment as work-attitude and institutional conditions rather than individual loyalty, positivity, attitude compliance, or retention-risk scoring.

## Research question

How can job satisfaction, organizational commitment, healthy attachment, constrained retention risk, symbolic commitment risk, and retention-system stress be represented, simulated, and reviewed as unit-level constructs shaped by leadership support, reward fairness, developmental opportunity, workload balance, institutional trust, cultural alignment, role insecurity, and exhaustion pressure?

## Conceptual assumptions

1. Job satisfaction is an evaluative response to work conditions, leadership, fairness, work design, meaning, workload, relationships, autonomy, and institutional trust.
2. Organizational commitment is multidimensional and can include affective, continuance, and normative components.
3. Retention is not always evidence of healthy attachment. It may reflect satisfaction, commitment, constraint, economic dependence, lack of alternatives, or fear of loss.
4. Low satisfaction may signal system conditions, not individual negativity.
5. Commitment should not be treated as silence, obedience, positivity, or unquestioning loyalty.
6. Work-attitude analytics must support institutional learning and better work design rather than worker scoring, surveillance, or employment decisions.

## Synthetic modeling aims

The scaffold demonstrates how to:

- generate job satisfaction and organizational commitment scores
- distinguish healthy attachment from constrained retention risk
- model retention risk as a unit-level or institutional signal
- identify symbolic commitment risk
- summarize work-attitude review priorities
- document responsible-use boundaries

## Appropriate interpretation

Outputs should be interpreted as prompts for unit, leadership, and institutional inquiry. A high-risk synthetic profile should not be read as a real-world diagnosis. In actual organizational research, quantitative signals require qualitative validation through stay interviews, exit interviews, workload review, promotion and compensation review, leadership assessment, psychological safety review, culture review, and participatory interpretation with affected groups.
